Office lighting design is a zoning and specification process, not the purchase of a single fixture. It maps tasks and zones across a floor plate, layers ambient, task, and circulation light, then ties each layer to metrics, controls, and fixture schedules that a B2B buyer can hold a supplier to.

![Open-plan office seen from a circulation aisle, with suspended linear lights over empty desk rows and recessed downlights in the aisle, and no people or text visible.](/insights%20image/insights%20feature%20images/office-lighting-design-open-plan-zoned.webp)

## What is office lighting design?

Office lighting design is a zoning and specification process: it starts with tasks and zones, assigns ambient, task, and circulation or accent layers, and then ties each layer to metrics, controls, and a fixture schedule. It is not the act of buying one fixture for an entire floor plate.

The three layers have different jobs. Ambient light sets the general visual field. Task light supports focused work at desks or tables. Circulation and accent light mark routes, reception, and selected features. A complete scheme describes all three, even if some rooms use a single fixture family to serve more than one role.

This article is the design and specification guide. Technical deep-dives on glare, color, controls, and fixture systems are linked rather than repeated, so the RFQ can stay focused on zones, metrics, and evidence a buyer can verify.

## Why does office lighting design matter for commercial projects?

Office lighting design matters because a commercial floor plate is not one visual task. Buyers need visual comfort, glare control, consistent zoning from floor to floor, and a controls plan that matches how the space is occupied. A scheme that looks tidy on a cut sheet can still fail if glare, color, or zoning drift between rooms.

Multi-floor projects also need a written zoning language so reception, desks, and back-of-house rooms do not get the same default package. Ask suppliers to price and submit against that schedule, and keep finish and CCT consistency in the same review.

Treat energy and controls as planning issues, not as promised savings. Specify zones, dimming, and sensors so the installed system can be operated as designed, without relying on unverified percentage claims.

For fixture-family context beyond the office floor plate, see our guide to [commercial led lighting](/insights/commercial-led-lighting-guide). This article stays on office zoning and the specification sheet.

## Which standards and metrics should you specify?

Name the documents the project will follow, then state metrics as project targets by zone. Illuminance, color, and glare should be written as requirements to verify, not as catalog slogans. These references set the visual brief; they are not claims about a factory catalog.

Consult EN 12464-1 and the CIE/ISO [Lighting of Work Places — Part 1: Indoor (ISO 8995-1)](https://www.cie.co.at/publications/lighting-work-places-part-1-indoor) for indoor workplace lighting, plus IES recommended practice for office facilities, and request IES photometric files for every shortlisted fixture.

Treat illuminance as a project target by zone, not as a generic catalog number. Do not copy a lux figure from another job without checking the standard edition and the local authority having jurisdiction.

Set glare or led ugr limits from the project standard, not from a supplier rule. For ugr office lighting background, see [What Is UGR in Office Lighting?](/insights/ugr-office-lighting-explained).

Color quality and appearance belong in the same brief. Many office interiors specify a cri 90 led source where faces, finishes, or materials must render faithfully; see [What Is CRI in Lighting?](/insights/what-is-cri-in-lighting) and [CRI explained](/insights/cri-explained). For a fixed-CCT choice, compare [3000k vs 4000k](/insights/3000k-vs-4000k-led), and use the [color temperature guide](/insights/color-temperature-guide) for broader context. Keep CCT consistent across adjacent zones unless a change is deliberate.

If a client also asks for circadian metrics or a WELL feature, treat that as an optional project requirement to verify, not as a product label. See [human centric lighting](/insights/human-centric-lighting-guide) rather than expanding that topic here.

## How should lighting change by office zone?

Lighting should change with the task, the room type, and how long people occupy the space. An open-plan desk field, a private office, a corridor, and a reception desk are different zones. Write those differences into the fixture schedule so substitutions cannot flatten the design into a single lighting condition.

In open-plan desk rows, use suspended linear ambient light in a uniform layout along the rows so desks share a consistent visual field. Keep glare control and layout alignment with the furniture grid in the same brief.

Meeting rooms need a flexible, dimmable ambient layer and a clear presentation condition. Keep this guidance brief and coordinate the room with the wider floor-plate CCT and CRI strategy rather than writing a separate lighting manual for each meeting space.

Private and cellular offices are a distinct zone from the open plan. Even ambient light from a surface or recessed panel usually serves a single desk and nearby walls, with less need for long linear runs.

Circulation routes call for downlights, lower-glare aiming, and a rhythm that supports wayfinding without competing with adjacent work areas. Reception can be calmer and more accented than the work floor, combining a quieter ambient layer with controlled highlights at the desk or feature wall.

Ancillary and back-of-house rooms are service-driven. Linkable battens are a practical choice where access, maintenance, and continuous runs matter more than a decorative ceiling.

## Which fixture types belong in an office lighting design?

Choose fixture types by the zone they serve, then confirm photometry, glare control, and controls before you lock the schedule. A clear family-to-zone map makes later substitutions easier to judge. Do not pick a workhorse fixture from wattage or appearance alone.

Suspended linear fixtures are the usual ambient layer for open-plan desks. A [linear bar office suspended light](/products/linear-bar-office-suspended-light) is a typical starting point; the [linear light guide](/insights/led-linear-light-guide) covers system options in more depth.

Panels suit regular ceiling grids and cellular rooms. Compare an [ultra-slim round surface panel](/products/ultra-slim-round-surface-panel-light) with a [square backlit LED surface panel](/products/square-backlit-led-surface-panel), and check shielding and driver compatibility.

Downlights serve circulation, reception, and perimeters. Options include a [trimless recessed COB downlight](/products/trimless-recessed-cob-downlight), a [classic recessed general downlight](/products/classic-recessed-general-downlight), an [adjustable gimbal LED downlight](/products/adjustable-gimbal-led-downlight), a [compact mini LED downlight IP44](/products/compact-mini-led-downlight-ip44), and a [modern cube surface ceiling downlight](/products/modern-cube-surface-ceiling-downlight). Use the [beam angle guide](/insights/beam-angle-guide-for-commercial-lighting) when circulation and accent distributions differ, and the [architectural LED lighting](/insights/architectural-led-lighting-guide) guide for perimeter or detail work.

Linkable battens belong in back-of-house and service rooms; see a [linear LED batten (linkable)](/products/linear-led-batten-light-linkable). Track and magnetic track are an accent or collaboration layer, not the open-plan workhorse. A [magnetic track light system](/products/magnetic-track-light-system-linear) can serve that role; system details are in the [commercial track light guide](/insights/commercial-track-light-guide).

## What should an office lighting design RFQ include?

An RFQ should turn the zoning plan into testable requirements so every bidder quotes the same scope. List rooms, tasks, and occupancy first, then attach visual targets, controls, and the evidence you will review. Keep substitutions subject to technical review before sampling or production.

1. Application and zones: floor plates, room types, tasks, and occupancy.
2. Visual targets by zone: illuminance, uniformity, CCT and CRI, and glare or UGR limits from the project standard.
3. Controls: dimming protocol, zoning, and sensors. Confirm compatibility with the [dimming guide](/insights/dimming-guide).
4. Photometric files: IES or LM reports for every SKU submitted.
5. Sample review and consistency checks across the fixture schedule.
6. Factory-side process questions, using the [LED lighting manufacturer guide](/insights/led-lighting-manufacturer-guide).
7. OEM or custom-interface needs, if required — see [OEM/ODM](/oem-odm).

## FAQ

### What is office lighting design?

Office lighting design is a zoning and specification process. It maps tasks and zones, layers ambient, task, and circulation light, and ties each layer to metrics, controls, and fixture schedules a B2B buyer can specify and verify.

### Which standards should I follow for office lighting?

Consult EN 12464-1 and ISO/CIE 8995-1 for indoor workplace lighting, and IES recommended practice for office facilities. These documents set the targets; confirm the edition and local adoption with your project's authority before finalizing the specification.

### What UGR or glare limit should I specify?

Glare limits come from your project standard, not from a single supplier rule. Set the UGR target in the RFQ and ask suppliers to submit photometric evidence. For background, see our UGR in office lighting explainer.

### Which fixtures are typically used in office lighting design?

Suspended linear fixtures for open-plan ambient, panels for grids and cellular rooms, downlights for circulation and reception, and linkable battens for back-of-house. Track and magnetic systems serve accent or collaboration layers, usually not the main workhorse.

### What should an office lighting RFQ include?

List application and zones, illuminance targets by zone, CCT and CRI, glare/UGR limits, controls and dimming protocol, and a requirement for IES/LM photometric files per SKU. Add sample review and schedule consistency checks before approval.
